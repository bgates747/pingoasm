# Development log — 2026-07-29

## Expanded single-model baselines

The existing Cube, EarthIco, and EarthUV fixture system was extended with
profile-driven RGBA2222 HeavyTank, Lara, Crash, Jet, and Airliner fixtures.
No assembly application was written model-by-model. The shared generator
produces all model includes, textures, absolute poses, and binaries.

Two independent workloads now exist for all eight models:

1. stationary Y-axis rotation; and
2. the qualified deterministic viewport/frustum sweep.

The shortened visual-review variants use no warmups and two rotations. All
models passed hardware visual review except the first Lara export, which was
upside down.

Lara's authoritative source was identified as
`src/blender/LaraCroft/LaraCroft.blend`. A new Blender 4 exporter produced
`LaraCroft.obj/.mtl` beside it using standard `-Z` forward, `+Y` up semantics
without historical renderer-compensation inversions. The fresh hardware
fixture rendered upright. Obsolete plain/root Lara exports and legacy
generated artifacts were moved recoverably to desktop trash.

## First multi-object scene

A separate profile-driven `benchmarks/orbit-scene` workload now renders
EarthUV with Crash, Lara, Jet, and Airliner orbiting it. This preserves every
single-model fixture and result as an independent baseline.

The deterministic scene initially used:

1. Earth at `(0,0,-1000)` and the camera at `(0,0,3200)`;
2. a 2,500-unit X-Z orbit with four 90-degree phase offsets;
3. two revolutions in 10-degree steps plus the exact closing pose;
4. three Earth Y rotations per orbit;
5. distinct half-integer XYZ spins that close only after two orbits;
6. three series of four warmups plus 73 measured frames; and
7. five independent RGBA2222 textures and one RGBA2222 render target.

The generator validates orbital closure, unique IDs, authoritative asset
dimensions, and the eZ80 executable-plus-staging-texture memory bound. The
assembled executable is 172,737 bytes; with its largest 262,144-byte texture,
the maximum eZ80 staging extent is 434,881 of 524,288 bytes.

The isolated current-Pingo emulator successfully uploaded all five textures,
created and rendered all five objects with mutual depth, completed the full
animation, restored video mode, and exited normally. Human visual review found
the result correct on the first run. This is emulator qualification only;
physical hardware remains the performance and final correctness authority.

Hard reasoning was used for the multi-object scene design and implementation.

### Denser orbit promoted

The initial 10-degree orbit fixture was superseded after visual review by the
2.5-degree version. The denser 289-pose sweep exposes substantially more
combinations of partial, complete, and absent object visibility and is
therefore the canonical fixture for upcoming culling experiments. It uses one
series and no warmups: its present purpose is exploratory renderer work and
visual fun, not strict comparative data capture.

The complete slow hardware review showed stable object identities and correct
motion. The earlier apparent model substitution was not reproduced and is
attributed to temporal aliasing and observer fatigue. The old 10-degree
measurement files remain only as historical evidence; the separate generated
10-degree fixture is no longer an active test target.

## Camera-motion exploration

The multi-object scene was extended with two independent, profile-driven
camera fixtures. Neither replaces the qualified object-orbit fixture.

### Linear camera dolly

The first fixture isolated absolute camera translation. Earth and its four
companions were initially held at fixed world poses while the camera moved
linearly on its Z axis:

```text
Z=10000 -> Z=2500 -> Z=10000
```

It used 289 poses, including both exact endpoints, and introduced no camera
rotation. Emulator review was visually correct. The original two-revolution
object choreography was then reinstated on the same timeline. The camera
completed one inward-and-outward oscillation while all object positions and
orientations returned exactly to their starting state. Both emulator and
hardware review were visually correct.

### Polar elliptical camera orbit

A separate fixture then exercised camera translation and rotation together.
Earth is one focus of an ellipse in the world Y-Z plane. The camera:

1. starts at `(0,0,10000)`, 11,000 units from Earth;
2. sweeps at constant angle rather than simulated orbital velocity;
3. passes over the north pole;
4. reaches periapsis 2,500 units beyond Earth's far side at `(0,0,-3500)`;
5. passes over the south pole; and
6. returns exactly to its starting position and orientation.

The radius is generated from:

```text
a = (apoapsis + periapsis) / 2
e = (apoapsis - periapsis) / (apoapsis + periapsis)
p = a(1 - e²)
r = p / (1 - e cos(theta))
```

The angular sweep is deliberately uniform; this is geometric test motion, not
Keplerian orbital mechanics. The camera completes one ellipse while the
objects complete two orbits.

An unrotated camera faces local `-Z`. On this polar path, a continuous X
rotation from 0 through -360 degrees always points that axis toward Earth.
Camera Y rotation and local roll remain zero. This choice is both simple and
important: at the polar crossings, camera-forward is parallel to the usual
world-up vector. A conventional `lookAt(position, target, world_up)` is
singular there. Prescribing the continuous X angle chooses the roll frame by
continuity while deliberately traversing the +/-90-degree pitch conditions
associated with Euler gimbal lock.

The TV-compatible assembly helper did not previously expose the absolute
three-axis camera-rotation command. `scrabs` was added using Pingo subcommand
21; an older helper elsewhere incorrectly labels a three-axis payload as
subcommand 18, which accepts only one X angle and must not be copied.

Eleven generator and mathematical regression tests now cover the canonical
orbit, camera dolly, elliptical endpoints, polar crossing, camera angles,
object motion, exact closure, validation, and eZ80 staging-memory bound. The
polar fixture assembled successfully and passed visual review in both the
qualified emulator and physical hardware. This establishes camera pose
translation and rotation—including smooth passage through both poles—as
working for this renderer state.
